The experiment-design workflow

Six decisions, in order — each has a calculator in this lab. The pedagogic companion to the study guide.

An online A/B test is a sequence of decisions made before you see a p-value: what to move, how big a test, how to read it, when to stop, and whether to trust it at all. Get these right and significance takes care of itself.

1Why this manual exists

The study guide covers estimators (DML, forests, interference). This lab covers operations — the part of platform experimentation that bites in production: tests are chronically underpowered, people peek, "significant" underpowered wins are exaggerated, and a silent SRM quietly invalidates everything. Each tab is a working calculator plus the one paper that justifies it.

Power, MDE & sample size

Online effects are tiny (median lift ~0.1–0.3%) — so a real test needs millions. Size it before you run it.

Four knobs trade off: the minimum detectable effect \(\blue\delta\), variance \(\sigma^2\), sample size \(n\), and power \(1-\beta\). Fix three, the fourth is forced. Variance reduction (CUPED) buys power for free by shrinking \(\sigma^2\).

The sample-size law (per arm, two-sided)
$$n_{\text{per arm}}=\frac{(z_{1-\alpha/2}+z_{1-\beta})^2\,2\sigma^2}{\blue{\delta}^2}\ \approx\ \frac{16\,\sigma^2}{\blue{\delta}^2}\quad(\alpha{=}.05,\ \text{power}{=}80\%;\ \text{Lehr's rule})$$

Binary uses \(\sigma^2=p(1-p)\) automatically; continuous takes your own \(\sigma\). MDE can be relative (% of baseline) or absolute.

1Choosing the MDE is the hard part

\(\blue\delta\) is not free — set it from a prior on effect sizes: A/B repositories, the company's average lift (~0.3%), or expert elicitation. Classical "small/medium/large" Cohen's-\(d\) thresholds don't transfer online — a "large" 5%-relative win is \(d\approx0.01\). Pick the smallest effect worth shipping, then trade it against duration.

⚠️ The replication crusher Kohavi's three ~2M-user replications shrank a published 55% CTR lift to ~0.2%, non-significant. A "significant" result from a small test is usually noise — see tabs 3 & 4.

2Variance reduction = free power

Because \(n\propto\sigma^2\), halving the variance halves the required sample. CUPED (regress out a pre-period covariate) and the DML/AIPW linear case do exactly this — never worse than naive. This is the cheapest power you will ever buy; it feeds the same calculator above through a smaller \(\sigma\).

Test & Roll — profit-maximizing sizing

Don't test for significance. Choose the test size that maximizes profit over a finite population, then roll the winner.

A decision-theoretic alternative to power analysis. Some test units get the worse arm (opportunity cost); a wrong call deploys the worse arm to the rest (deployment-error cost). The optimal test balances the two — and is far smaller than NHST.

Profit-maximizing test size (per arm)
$$n_1^*=n_2^*=\sqrt{\frac{N}{4}\Big(\frac{s}{\sigma}\Big)^2+\frac34\Big(\frac{s}{\sigma}\Big)^4}-\frac34\Big(\frac{s}{\sigma}\Big)^2\ \le\ \frac{\sqrt N\,s}{2\sigma}$$

\(N\) = finite population · \(s\) = response-noise SD · \(\sigma\) = prior SD of the mean response. Decision rule = pick the winner, no threshold.

1Why the test comes out tiny

  • Scales with \(\sqrt N\) — and is always \(
  • Sub-linear in noise — grows with \(s\), not \(s^2\); noisy responses give smaller tests (where NHST often demands \(n>N\), Lewis–Rao).
  • Decreases with \(\sigma\) — more prior uncertainty / bigger expected gap ⇒ a smaller test settles it.
  • Near-bandit — regret \(O(\sqrt N)\) with only two allocation decisions and a closed form.
  • Asymmetric priors ⇒ unequal arms / tiny holdouts — rationalizes the "1% holdout" practice.

2Prior-free: the rule of thirds

Test & Roll needs a prior \((\mu,\sigma)\). Kawato–Sakaguchi remove it via welfare-aware worst-case marginal benefit: add matched pairs while the worst-case benefit of one more exceeds its exploration cost. The answer is clean —

$$m^*\approx\frac{N}{3}\qquad(\text{test }\sim\tfrac13,\ \text{roll }\sim\tfrac23)$$

exact for Gaussian known-variance, a good approximation for Bernoulli. Plug-and-play when you have no prior.

🎯 Vega hook — the ramp is test-and-roll A/A → 1% → 5% → 20% → 50% → ship is "test on a subset, roll the winner." OEC = profit = Vega's primary metric (margin per active user), so reward-sizing is literally a test-and-roll decision. With ground truth we can measure the profit gap between this sizing and NHST sizing.

The winner's curse — Type S & Type M

An underpowered and significant estimate is exaggerated, and may even have the wrong sign.

Power isn't only about detection. Conditional on clearing significance, a low-powered estimate overstates the true effect (Type-M, magnitude) and can flip its sign (Type-S). This is the winner's curse that makes small "wins" un-replicable.

Conditional-on-significance distortions
$$\text{Type-M}=\frac{E\big[\,|\hat\delta|\ \big|\ \text{significant}\big]}{|\blue\delta|},\qquad \text{Type-S}=P\big(\text{wrong sign}\ \big|\ \text{significant}\big)$$

1How bad it gets

Gelman–Carlin's running example: at 3% power the expected exaggeration is ~28×; at 2.55% power, >200×. So a barely-significant finding from a tiny study doesn't just risk a false positive — when it is real, it reports an effect many times too large (Button et al. 2013, "Power failure").

💡 The mechanism Significance is a filter: \(|\hat\delta|>z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\mathrm{SE}\). When power is low, only the luckiest upward noise draws survive the filter — so the surviving estimates are, by construction, inflated. Raising power shrinks the gap to 1×.

2Small telescopes (Simonsohn 2015)

The replication question isn't "does it match the original result?" but "was the original design informative?" Define \(d_{33}\) = the effect that would have given the original study 33% power; if the replication effect \(\ll d_{33}\), the original telescope was too small to see anything. Replication \(n\approx 2.5\times\) the original.

🎯 Vega hook Because Vega knows ground-truth \(\tau\), "underpowered-significant overstates the effect" becomes a measured, asserted quantity — a test, exactly like the naive-bias demonstration. Report Type-M alongside every readout, never just \(p<.05\).

False-positive risk — "significant" ≠ "true"

With a low base rate of real effects, a p<0.05 result is wrong far more than 5% of the time.

\(\alpha\) is \(P(\text{significant}\mid H_0)\) — not \(P(H_0\mid\text{significant})\). Bayes' rule with the base rate \(\pi\) of true effects gives the number that actually matters: how often a "win" is nothing.

False-positive risk (Bayesian posterior on the null)
$$\text{FPR}=P(H_0\mid\text{significant})=\frac{(1-\pi)\,\alpha}{(1-\pi)\,\alpha+\pi\,(1-\beta)}$$

Median experiment success rate is ~10% (\(\pi=0.1\)). Watch FPR explode as power drops or the base rate falls.

1What to do about it

  • Raise power — the cheapest lever; FPR is decreasing in \(1-\beta\).
  • Lower \(\alpha\) — Benjamin et al. 2017 propose \(\alpha=0.005\) for new discoveries.
  • Raise the prior \(\pi\) — test hypotheses with real mechanism, not 200 cosmetic tweaks.
  • Decide Bayesianly — report the posterior, not a thresholded p-value.
⚠️ Ratio metrics Randomize-by-user but analyze-by-event and the naive variance is wrong → mis-stated power and FPR. Use the Delta method (Deng–Knoblich–Lu 2018) for ratio metrics; Fieller's theorem for ratio CIs.

Peeking & anytime-valid inference

Continuous monitoring with fixed-horizon p-values drives Type-I error toward 100%. The fix lives in the time/data dimension.

A fixed-horizon p-value is valid at one pre-committed \(n\). Reject the first time \(p_t\le\alpha\) and, by the law of the iterated logarithm, you are guaranteed to cross any fixed \(\alpha\) eventually. Anytime-valid methods control error simultaneously over all \(t\).

2Always-valid p-values & confidence sequences

A sequence \((p_t)\) is always-valid if for any stopping time \(T\): \(P_{\theta_0}(p_T\le s)\le s\). The dual object is a confidence sequence — intervals with time-uniform coverage:

$$P\big(\forall t\ge 1:\ \theta\in CI_t\big)\ge 1-\alpha\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\text{safe to peek/stop anytime}$$

Concrete sub-Gaussian CS for the mean (Howard et al. 2022) — half-width relative to the fixed-\(n\) CI:

$$\underbrace{1.7\,\sigma\sqrt{\tfrac{\log\log(2t)+0.72\log(10.4/\alpha)}{t}}}_{\text{CS half-width}}\quad\text{vs}\quad\underbrace{z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\sigma/\sqrt t}_{\text{fixed-}n}$$

The extra \(\log\log t\) is the unavoidable price of peeking — width still \(\to 0\), just at the LIL rate. The chart shows the tax:

4Peeking 2.0 — within-unit peeking

The subtler hazard: peeking at a unit before all its measurements are in. With repeated measures per user the within-unit covariance is unmodeled (inflated FPR) and the estimand itself shifts between looks. Fix: a longitudinal GLS regression \(y_{it}=\beta_0+\delta\,d_{it}+\varepsilon_{it}\), then a GST exploiting the estimator's independent-increments property.

🎯 Vega hook Agents emit many events over the horizon, so reading the event log sequentially is within-unit peeking. 5Fixed-power — "it's not if you peek, it's what you peek at"

Nordin–Schultzberg 2024: not all stopping rules are equally harmful. Peeking at the variance function (to estimate the required \(N\)) barely hurts; peeking at significance badly does. So estimate \(N\) from accruing data, stop when reached, and analyze with ordinary non-sequential methods — no correction. Bonus: rules that stop on significance bias the effect upward (the Type-M curse, tab 3).

SRM & trust guardrails

If the realized split doesn't match the design, stop — the experiment is untrustworthy, full stop.

A Sample Ratio Mismatch means the assignment mechanism is broken (bot filtering, redirects, logging races). No clever analysis survives it. Test the realized counts against the designed ratio; a minuscule p-value is a hard stop, not a curiosity.

SRM test (\(\chi^2\), 1 d.o.f.)
$$\chi^2=\sum_{a\in\{T,C\}}\frac{(n_a-E_a)^2}{E_a},\qquad E_T=\rho\,(n_T{+}n_C),\ \ E_C=(1-\rho)(n_T{+}n_C)$$

1Common causes & the eBay leg

SRM is the canary for the whole trust stack: broken randomization, bot/scraper traffic in one arm, redirect latency, delayed logging, carryover from a prior test.
